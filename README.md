**Use this when** you need a resume and you have real but unimpressive looking
experience, and you want it written up without anything made up.

## The rule

Every line on this resume traces to something you said. If you did not say it,
it is not on the page. Not a softer version of it, not a reasonable inference
from it, not a standard duty of that job title.

That sounds obvious and it is not what usually happens. The usual failure is
quiet: you say "I worked at a restaurant," and what comes back is "trained new
staff, managed cash handling, resolved customer escalations." Plausible. Common
for that job. You never said any of it. Now you are sitting across from someone
who asks how you trained people, and you are either lying or confused about
your own resume.

## How it works

**1. You tell me what you did, loosely.** Jobs, clubs, school projects, things
you did for family or neighbors that were real work. Unpolished is fine.

**2. I write the lines, and every one carries a tag.**

| tag | means |
|---|---|
| `FROM YOU` | you said it, and I quote the words I built the line from |
| `CHECKED` | a document you pasted confirms it, a syllabus, an offer letter, a screenshot |
| `GUESSED` | I inferred it, and it does not ship until you confirm or kill it |

**3. Your job title stays exactly what it was.** Not an upgraded version. If
your title was weak and your work was not, the work goes in the bullet and the
title stays accurate. A reader can tell the difference and will respect it. A
reader who catches an inflated title stops believing the rest of the page.

**4. Numbers only if you gave one or can get one.** No percentage, no headcount,
no dollar figure, no "improved efficiency by" anything unless it came from you.
Where a number would help, the line ships without it and I leave a bracket
telling you exactly what to look up and where.

A bullet with a real action and no number is fine. An invented number is the
one thing on a resume that can end a candidacy on the spot.

## The trap

A naive version fills gaps with the job description instead of your job. It
knows what a cashier generally does, what a club treasurer generally does, and
it writes that, because the result looks complete and reads well. You then skim
it, recognize the shape of your own life, and approve it. Nobody lied on
purpose. The page is still partly fiction, and the interview is where you find
out which parts.

The second trap is the resume voice itself. "Spearheaded," "leveraged,"
"streamlined" smuggle in scope you never had. A verb that implies you ran
something you participated in is an invented claim in disguise.

## Limits

It cannot make thin experience thick. If you worked one summer, this produces an
honest one summer resume, and that is the correct output. Padding it is the
problem it exists to prevent.

It cannot verify a single thing you tell me. `CHECKED` only means a document you
handed over says so.

It does not know your field's conventions. Academic CVs, trades, design
portfolios, and federal applications all have their own expected format. Ask
someone who hires in your field what shape they expect, then hold the content
to the standard here.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
